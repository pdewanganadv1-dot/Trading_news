Why the Auction Blasts

The close jumped 151 points on 4 August and barely 11 on the 18th. The difference is not on the chart — it is in the order book. Get the mechanism right and the prediction stops being technical analysis.

The move is imbalance divided by depth

Between 15:20 and 15:30 the whole day's closing price is decided in one auction. Almost nobody is in it: SEBI's own order on the 13 August episode put the auction turnover at under 1% of the day's cash volume. Price in an auction is wherever buy and sell quantities balance, so in a book that thin, a modest one-sided order pushes the equilibrium a long way. The same net imbalance that would move nothing during the day moves the close hard here. That is the entire phenomenon — everything below is about what creates the imbalance, and how much of it you can see before the options stop trading.

What creates the imbalance on an expiry day

Four things push the auction book off balance, in rough order of how directly they cause the move:

1 · settlement & hedging — tied to expiring open interest

Index options settle on the CAS close, so every rupee of open interest that finishes in-the-money is a cash flow struck at that one price. Dealers who are short those options are delta-hedged, and as expiry certainty snaps to 0 or 1 through the auction their hedge has to be closed at the close — a wall of market-on-close orders whose direction is set by where the open interest sits versus spot. This is the piece you can measure before 15:15.

2 · passive & rebalance flow — calendar-known

Index funds and ETFs must trade at the official close to track their benchmark, and on monthly-expiry and index-reconstitution dates those baskets are large and one-directional. A monthly expiry carries heavier, more concentrated close orders than an ordinary weekly.

3 · manipulation — the thin book invites it

On 13 August one entity placed large buy orders across every Sensex stock and cancelled most, another sold eight stocks down for five minutes and pulled its orders after booking option profits; SEBI put the engineered move near +250 points and banned both. You cannot predict a spoof — but the fact that ₹3 crore of fake orders could move a whole index is the thin-book thesis stated in the negative.

4 · the day's trend — a weak proxy, not a cause

Across the eight expiries traded so far, the bigger the day's own high-low range, the bigger the auction move (correlation ≈ 0.78 on the real prints; ≈ 0.79 with the manipulated day excluded). But a trending day both leaves more directional OI to unwind and reflects the same one-way pressure — the range is downstream of the imbalance, not its source. With eight days and one of them manipulated, treat it as a hint, never the signal.

The CAS expiries, read through the mechanism

13 August is marked because SEBI found it manipulated — its +225 (real prints, 15:10 bar to the CAS close) is engineered, not organic, and it should be excluded from any honest model even though it was the fattest option payoff of the month. Setting it aside, the biggest moves were the first CAS weekly on each exchange (4 Aug NIFTY +0.62%, 6 Aug SENSEX +0.22%), when positioning was largest and the book least settled, and the NIFTY monthly on the 25th (+0.31%). The BANKNIFTY monthly on the same day moved just +0.10% — a monthly label alone does not make a blast — and the ordinary mid-cycle weeklies with balanced books all closed within ±0.09%. Seven of the eight auction moves were upward, and six of the eight went toward max-pain.

So the predictor gets rebuilt

The buy-score in the rest of this toolkit reads the daytime chart — breakouts, ranges, a volatility score. Those are proxies for imbalance, and weak ones: they were the best available with no option data. Once real open interest is in hand, the prediction should lean on the cause, not the shadow. Three tiers, by when you can see them:

A · known by 15:10 — tradeable

Where the expiring OI sits relative to spot (max-pain distance, the share of OI within a strike of spot, how concentrated it is), whether it is a monthly or a reconstitution date, and the pre-auction trend. Options are still trading, so a signal here is actionable before the freeze. This is the tier scripts/cas_auction_anatomy.py builds from the Dhan OI history.

B · the live imbalance — 15:20–15:30

The exchange broadcasts the indicative equilibrium price, cumulative buy/sell quantities and the imbalance in real time through the auction. This is the imbalance itself, not a proxy — the strongest possible signal. The catch is timing: whether you can still buy the option after reading it depends on the exact minute index derivatives stop, which the current rules and SEBI's 23 August reform proposal are both still settling. Capture it; treat its tradeability as an open question to confirm, not an assumption.

C · calendar & regime — known days ahead

Monthly vs weekly, index rebalance effective dates, and — bluntly — how thin and young the book is. The first weeks of CAS produced the biggest organic moves; as participation deepens the same imbalance will move price less. The edge itself has a shelf life.

The reframing in one line: stop asking "will the index trend into the close" and start asking "is the auction book set up to be pushed, and which way" — a question about open interest and flow, answered mostly before 15:15 and confirmed by the imbalance broadcast, not by a candlestick.

What the Dhan run measures

Every feature below is model-free — computed from traded prints and real 15:10 open interest, on the CAS expiries only. scripts/cas_auction_anatomy.py now emits them for each real expiry (the table above carries the measured values); as expiries accumulate, this is what the prediction gets fitted on, in place of the chart features.

  • max-pain distance — 15:10 spot minus the max-pain strike, signed: the direction and pull of the pin.
  • ATM OI share — how much open interest sits within one strike of spot, where it can flip in- or out-of-the-money in the auction.
  • OI concentration — spread out across strikes, or piled on a few (a thin, movable structure).
  • monthly / rebalance flag — the passive-flow multiplier.
  • realized auction move — 15:10 to CAS close, the target — plus, when captured, the live imbalance at 15:25 as the tier-B check.

With eight expiries this is a hypothesis with the plumbing built, not a fitted model — a handful of days cannot separate four causes, and one of the eight is a spoof. The first real read cuts both ways: the OI anatomy barely varied across the month (concentration ~0.16–0.19 on every day, ATM share 34–60%), and the day with the heaviest ATM share (11 Aug, 60%) produced one of the smallest moves — so nothing here separates big days from quiet ones yet. The value is that every new expiry pulled through Dhan drops into a frame that already knows what to measure and why.

Does the edge fade as CAS matures?

Yes — and the regulator agrees, which is the part worth taking seriously. Asked about the sharp closing moves, SEBI called them a maturing-market effect, not a design flaw, and said it expects them to ease as participation rises — auction participation already grew from day one to day two after the 3 August launch. That is the imbalance-÷-depth mechanism running in reverse: as more players join the auction, the book thickens, and the same expiry flow moves the close less. No immediate change to CAS is planned; the derivative-stock reform (a ±3% band, live imbalance data) is at consultation, not in force.

How accurate can this actually get

Straight answer: this is not going to be a crystal ball, and any tool that claims to call the close to the point is lying to you. The honest target is narrower and still useful — separating the days the auction is likely to move a lot from the days it won't, and, on the big-move days, leaning the right way more often than a coin. Here is what bounds it.

magnitude — the learnable part

Whether the auction will be big or quiet is tied to structure you can see by 15:10: concentrated OI, a heavy ATM, a monthly, a thin young book. A model can rank days on this. Realistically that is an AUC in the 0.60–0.70 band if the edge is real — meaningfully better than guessing, nowhere near certainty.

direction — mostly not learnable

Which way the auction breaks is driven by fresh order flow that hasn't arrived yet at 15:10, and occasionally by a spoof. The max-pain pull leans it, weakly. Expect direction accuracy close to a coin — the honest play on a big-move day is often to buy both sides (the strangle in the timetable), not to bet the way.

irreducible — never learnable

13 August was moved by fake orders no feature could have predicted; genuinely new institutional flow is unknowable by construction. A slice of the auction move is noise, and no amount of data removes it. The goal is to harvest the structured part, not to pretend the noise isn't there.

The one thing that makes a future number trustworthy

An accuracy number is worthless unless the way it was measured cannot fool itself. So the model is judged by strict walk-forward — each expiry predicted using only earlier ones, never the future — and then against a permutation null: the same walk-forward re-run 200 times on scrambled targets, and the real result only counts as an edge if it beats that noise band (p < 0.05). So the path to accuracy, honestly: the direction bet stays humble; the magnitude read is where the edge lives; and no number ships until strict walk-forward beats the permutation null. Two data streams feed it — the full-history pull (~45 weekly expiries since Sept 2025) tests the OI→close-drift link today as a leading indicator, and the CAS expiries accumulate the real auction target at roughly one a week. A trustworthy CAS-specific model is months of Tuesdays and Thursdays away — but you will know when it arrives, because the harness will say so instead of guessing.
